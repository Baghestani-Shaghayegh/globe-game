# How GuessGlobe talks

Rules for any words a player reads: buttons, prompts, feedback, cards, empty
states. Written after Sara pointed out that "Five neighbouring countries at a
time: meet them, find them, name them. No clock, no score." read like a chatbot
wrote it, not a game.

## The rules

1. **Say the thing, not a description of the feature.** A game shows what to
   do; it doesn't pitch itself. "Where's Andorra?" beats "Click on the globe
   to find the country."
2. **Use the real content.** Country names, numbers, directions. "That's
   Portugal. Head north-east." is something a player can act on. "Not quite,
   try again!" isn't.
3. **Short enough to read mid-game.** Players skim or skip text, so a
   prompt is a few words and feedback is one line. If the globe or a colour
   already says it, don't say it again in words.
4. **Buttons say what happens next.** "Play Europe 4 →", "Find them →",
   "Next →". Not "Continue", "Submit" or "Get started".
5. **One voice everywhere.** The same verb for the same action: a typed answer
   is a **Guess** in every mode, a finished lesson is **Done**.
6. **Don't explain what's missing.** No clock and no score is shown by there
   being none; it doesn't need a sentence.

## Patterns that make text read as AI-written

Avoid these; they are the patterns people now recognise:

- **Lists of exactly three**, especially parallel verbs: "meet them, find
  them, name them". Use one, or two, or four.
- **"No X, no Y."** and **"It's not X, it's Y."** — the made-up contrast.
- **Puns and idioms as headings**: "Lesson learned", "The world is your
  oyster".
- **Puffed-up words**: journey, unlock, master, discover, explore, delve,
  seamless, elevate, "take your … to the next level".
- **Tidy two-beat slogans**: "Start small. Think big."
- **Em dashes to join clauses in UI text.** Fine in long prose; in a button
  or a one-line message, use a full stop.
- **Cheerleading that could go under any answer**: "Amazing!", "Great job!".
  Name the thing instead: "✓ Andorra".
